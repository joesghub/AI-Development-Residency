### **Methodology Behind the Market Selection**

I started with the core problem the product solves: helping people with ADHD learn to budget. 

That problem has two halves, a money problem and an executive-function problem. 

So I looked for markets where companies already solve one half, the other half, or both. Each market had to pass four tests:

1. **Same problem:** companies in the market help people manage money or manage ADHD.
2. **Same users:** the market reaches adults who could become customers of an ADHD budgeting app.
3. **Distinct business model:** each market makes money differently, so together they show the different ways the product could earn revenue (consumer subscriptions, health and wellness spending, employer benefits).
4. **Measurable:** published industry research exists to size it, so the numbers can be defended.

### Four markets passed

- **Personal finance & budgeting apps** is the direct market. These are the apps a person with ADHD would try today and the ones the product would compete with head-on.
- **ADHD & neurodivergent support apps** is the needs-based market. These companies already serve the exact audience and have earned their trust, but they don’t address money.
- **Employer financial wellness benefits** is the channel-based market. It describes a different buyer, an employer paying on behalf of employees, rather than a different product. I included it because it is a realistic second path to revenue.
- **ADHD-specific money management** is the intersection, where the product would actually live. I included it to show that this niche has no established leader, which is the basis of the market opportunity.
- I deliberately left out several related markets:
    - **Banks and neobanks**, because budgeting is a side feature for them rather than their product, and their market size would distort the comparison.
    - **General mental health apps** such as Headspace or Calm, because they are too broad and don’t touch finances.
    - **Robo-advisors and investing apps**, because they serve people who already have money to invest rather than people learning to budget.
    - **Business budgeting software**, because it serves companies, not individuals.

## **Market Opportunity**

The strongest opportunity sits where the personal finance market and the ADHD app market overlap. Both are large and growing, but almost no one is serving the overlap directly.

The audience is large and still growing. In 2023, an estimated 15.5 million U.S. adults had an ADHD diagnosis, about half of whom were diagnosed in adulthood. That means millions of people are learning to manage ADHD for the first time as adults, often while already handling their own rent, bills, and debt. Many are also unsupported: 36.5% of adults with ADHD received no treatment in the previous year. Money stress is part of the picture too, since adults earning below the federal poverty level were more likely to be diagnosed (22.1% versus 12.3%).

The gap is in how existing products are designed. Mainstream budgeting apps like YNAB and Monarch assume users can do the very things ADHD makes hardest: categorize every transaction, check in on a regular schedule, plan ahead, and avoid impulse purchases. 

ADHD apps, meanwhile, help with focus, tasks, and routines, but almost none address money, even though impulsive spending, missed bills, and late fees are common pain points for people with ADHD. 

So users either drop budgeting apps after a few weeks or never find a tool built for how their brains work.

The opportunity is therefore a budgeting app built around executive function rather than discipline. Its main traits would be:

- **Low effort:** automation and minimal manual entry.
- **Timely help:** nudges that arrive when spending decisions actually happen.
- **Visual and motivating:** feedback that is visual, immediate, and rewarding.
- **Shame-free:** no judgment for missed days or slip-ups.

It's a good sign that the ADHD app market is growing faster than general finance apps, about 17% versus about 11%, and that the niche has no dominant player yet. 

Employer financial wellness benefits also offer a second route to revenue: selling the app as a neuro-inclusive benefit to companies that want to support neurodivergent employees.

## Competitor Analysis Prep

### **Step 1: Questions to ask about competitors**

Good competitor questions are specific to your product’s main bet. Yours is that people with ADHD fail at budgeting because existing tools demand executive-function skills they struggle with. So the most useful questions test whether competitors design for that or not. Generic questions like “what’s their pricing?” are already covered by other columns in your table.

Here are candidate questions grouped by the ADHD challenge they probe:

- **Effort and friction:** How much manual work does the app require, such as categorizing every transaction or doing weekly reviews? How long does setup take before a user gets value?
- **Re-engagement:** What happens when a user stops using the app for a week or a month? Does it help them restart, or does it pile up overdue tasks and make them feel behind?
- **Impulse spending:** Does the app help in the moment a spending decision happens, or only afterward in reports?
- **Motivation and tone:** Does the app use rewards, visuals, or gamification? Does its messaging feel shame-free, or does it lean on discipline and guilt?
- **ADHD awareness:** Does the company acknowledge ADHD or neurodivergent users at all in its marketing, features, or content?
- **Bills and deadlines:** Does it actively prevent missed bills and late fees, a common ADHD pain point, or just track them?

### **Step 2: How to find direct competitors manually**

Use several sources so you don’t just list the apps you’ve already heard of:

1. **App store searches:** Search the Apple App Store and Google Play for terms your audience would actually type, such as “budget app,” “ADHD budget,” “ADHD money,” and “spending tracker.” Note the top 10 results for each term, plus their ratings and review counts.
2. **Review roundups:** Search “best budgeting apps 2026” and “best budgeting apps for ADHD.” Apps that appear across several independent roundups are the established players.
3. **Community research:** Search Reddit (for example r/ADHD and r/personalfinance) for “ADHD budgeting app.” This shows what your target users actually use and complain about, which is often different from what review sites recommend. It may also turn up small ADHD-specific budgeting apps you’d otherwise miss.
4. **App store reviews:** Read the 1- to 3-star reviews of the top apps and search within them for “ADHD.” These reviews feed straight into the “What do they not do well?” column.
5. **Market reports:** Check the companies named in the research you already gathered for the Market Segments table.

### **Step 3: Sort and record what you find**

For each competitor, fill in the following, in the same order as the table columns:

1. **Competitor:** Record the product name, plus the company name if it's different.
2. **Indirect or Direct?** Sort each one using these definitions:
    - **Direct:** the app's main purpose is helping individuals budget.
    - **Indirect:** the app serves the same audience or need differently. This includes substitutes such as spreadsheets or pen and paper.
3. **Market Segment:** Match the competitor to one of the segments from your market research: personal finance and budgeting apps, ADHD and neurodivergent support apps, ADHD-specific money management, or manual budgeting (substitutes).
4. **Website:** Copy the URL from the official site or app store listing, not from a review site.
5. **Main Features and Capabilities:** List the core features from the company's own homepage and app store description.
6. **How do they differentiate themselves?** Note the main claim the company makes about why it's different, usually found in its homepage headline or app store tagline.
7. **What do they do well?** Note the strengths users and reviewers praise most, drawn from 4- and 5-star reviews and review roundups.
8. **What do they not do well?** Note the weaknesses that come up repeatedly, drawn from 1- to 3-star reviews, Reddit threads, and review site "cons" lists.
9. **Q1: Fall-off recovery:** How does the product support users when they fall off track? Look for re-engagement features, catch-up tools, and tone, such as whether it is shame-free or guilt-based.
10. **Q2: Effort required:** How much ongoing manual effort does the product require? Rate it Low, Medium, or High based on setup, categorizing, and how often reviews are needed.
11. **Q3: In-the-moment help:** Does it help at the moment of a spending decision, or only afterward? Look for real-time alerts, safe-to-spend numbers, and purchase cooldowns, as opposed to after-the-fact reports.

## **Opportunities**

Reading across the three question columns shows a clear pattern. No competitor does well on all three:

- **YNAB** has the strongest ADHD marketing and a shame-free tone, but its method demands high effort, the very thing ADHD makes hard.
    - **This is a key finding**: *your biggest direct competitor already targets your audience, so your product has to beat it on effort, not just messaging.*
- **PocketGuard and Rocket Money** are low-effort but offer no habit-building or emotional support, and little help with impulse spending.
- **Rule** is the closest to your concept, with ADHD-specific design, gamification, and impulse cooldowns. But it's early-stage and UK-based, which leaves the U.S. market open.
- **Inflow and Tiimo** have earned ADHD users' trust, but they don't touch money at all.

The unmet need is a single U.S. product that combines all three:

1. **Low effort:** automation like PocketGuard's.
2. **In-the-moment impulse support:** like Rule's cooldowns.
3. **Shame-free fall-off recovery:** a coaching and motivation layer like Inflow's.

That combination is the gap your product can fill, and it gives you the foundation for your unique value proposition and positioning statements in the next part of the assignment.

## Step 1: Target Audience

**Who is the product for?**

U.S. adults with ADHD, aged 50 and under, who use or want to try a budgeting app.

- **Size:** about 3.4–6.0 million people (see Market Size section)
- **Why this group:** it's the only segment where the product fits the problem exactly and no competitor clearly owns the space

## Step 2: What the Audience Cares About

**What problems are we trying to solve for audiences?**

| Problem | Why It Matters for ADHD |
| --- | --- |
| **Budgeting takes too much effort** | Existing tools require daily categorizing, regular reviews, and planning ahead, the exact executive-function tasks ADHD makes hardest. Users abandon them within weeks. |
| **Impulse spending happens in the moment** | Most tools only report spending afterward, when it's too late to change the decision. |
| **Falling off track means quitting for good** | A missed week creates a backlog and a sense of failure, and the guilt keeps users from returning. |

**What they value:** low effort, immediate feedback, flexibility, and tools that don't make them feel judged.

## Step 3: Compare with Competitors

**Which competitors are solving the same problems, and where are the gaps?**

| Competitor | Known For | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **YNAB** | A proven budgeting method; actively markets to ADHD users | Depends on frequent manual effort; returning after time away means clearing a backlog |
| **PocketGuard** | Automated tracking and a simple safe-to-spend number | No motivation, impulse intervention, or ADHD support |
| **Rule** | ADHD-specific gamified saving with impulse cooldowns | Early-stage, UK-based, and lacks core budgeting tools like bill reminders |
| **Inflow** | CBT-based ADHD skills and support for recovering from setbacks | No money features |

**Status quo:** most people still budget with pen and paper (37.3%) or spreadsheets (27.4%), and non-budgeters' top reason for skipping it is that it's too time-consuming (34.2%).

## Step 4: Find Our Unique Spot

**What impact will our solution have on audience problems?**

[Product Name] keeps people with ADHD budgeting long enough to build lasting habits. It removes the effort that causes abandonment, steps in before impulse purchases instead of reporting them afterward, and makes coming back after time away instant and guilt-free.

|  | Competitors' Impact | Our Impact |

## Step 6: Unique Attributes

*These are current assumptions based on the competitive and market analysis, to be validated with users.*

- **Automation-first:** the app does the tracking and remembering, so users don't have to.
- **Intervenes before the purchase:** cooldowns and pause prompts at the moment of a spending decision, not reports after it.
- **Designed for comebacks:** returning after time away is instant and guilt-free, with no backlog to clear.
- **Motivating by design:** rewards and visual progress built for the ADHD brain's need for immediate feedback.

No single competitor offers all four. The feature analysis showed that no competitor pairs a safe-to-spend view with impulse intervention, and only Rule pairs impulse intervention with gamification.
