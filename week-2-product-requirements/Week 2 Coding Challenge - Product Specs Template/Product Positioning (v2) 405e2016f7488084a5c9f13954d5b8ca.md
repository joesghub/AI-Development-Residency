## Step 1: Target Audience

**Who is the product for?**

U.S. adults with ADHD, aged 50 and under, who use or want to try a budgeting app.

- **Size:** about 3.4–6.0 million people (see Market Size section)
- **Why this group:** it's the only segment where the product fits the problem exactly and no competitor clearly owns the space

## Step 2: What the Audience Cares About

**What problems are we trying to solve for audiences?**

| Problem | Why It Matters for ADHD |
| --- | --- |
| **Budgeting takes too much effort** | Existing tools require daily categorizing, regular reviews, and planning ahead, the exact executive-function tasks ADHD makes hardest. Users abandon them within weeks. |
| **Wants are handled with willpower or regret** | Budgets either expect users to resist in the moment, which relies on impulse control, or show overspending afterward, which brings guilt. Neither helps people with ADHD get the things they want without blowing their budget. |
| **Falling off track means quitting for good** | A missed week creates a backlog and a sense of failure, and the guilt keeps users from returning. |

**What they value:** low effort, immediate and visible rewards, flexibility, and tools that don't make them feel judged.

## Step 3: Compare with Competitors

**Which competitors are solving the same problems, and where are the gaps?**

| Competitor | Known For | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **YNAB** | A proven budgeting method; actively markets to ADHD users, including a manual wish list category | Wish lists are manual budget categories with no scheduled dates or reminders; the method depends on frequent manual effort; returning after time away means clearing a backlog |
| **PocketGuard** | Automated tracking and a simple safe-to-spend number | No link between restraint and future rewards; no motivation or ADHD support |
| **Rule** | ADHD-specific gamified saving with impulse cooldowns | Early-stage and UK-based; relies on resisting in the moment; lacks core budgeting tools like bill reminders |
| **Inflow** | CBT-based ADHD skills and support for recovering from setbacks | No money features |

**Status quo:** most people still budget with pen and paper (37.3%) or spreadsheets (27.4%), and non-budgeters' top reason for skipping it is that it's too time-consuming (34.2%).

## Step 4: Find Our Unique Spot

**What impact will our solution have on audience problems?**

Patiencents turns budgeting from a test of willpower into a plan users look forward to. Every day they spend less than Today's Number, the leftover money (confirmed by their bank) goes into the Patience Pot and brings the next wishlist item's Buy Day closer. Wants aren't denied, just scheduled. When users slip or step away, nothing piles up, so coming back is instant.

## Step 5: Our Impact vs. Competitors' Impact

**How is our business's impact different from competitors' impact?**

|  | Competitors' Impact | Patiencents' Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who benefits** | People who can already stick with budgeting, or people with ADHD in general | People with ADHD who have struggled to stick with budgeting |
| **How wants are handled** | Cut, resisted, or regretted | Planned and celebrated: "bought on purpose" |
| **What changes** | Budgets become more detailed, or ADHD symptoms are better managed overall | Users keep budgeting, spend more steadily, and buy what they want on purpose |
| **How success is measured** | Budget accuracy, features used | Long-term engagement, Patience Pot growth, and purchases made on Buy Days instead of on impulse |

## Step 6: Unique Attributes

*These are current assumptions based on the competitive analysis and user journeys, to be validated with users.*

- **Not no, just later:** wants go on a wishlist and get a Buy Day when the budget can cover them. No competitor schedules purchases automatically.
- **Patience you can see:** unspent money goes into the Patience Pot and visibly moves Buy Days closer, connecting today's restraint to tomorrow's reward.
- **Every number is real:** rewards are confirmed by bank data, never self-reported, so users can trust their progress.
- **The app comes to you:** a morning notification with Today's Number and the next Buy Day countdown, so users never have to remember to check.
- **Built for comebacks:** missed Buy Days become "Kept your patience," overspending never reduces the Pot, and returning after time away takes 10 seconds.

## Brand

| Element | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Name** | Patiencents (patience + cents) |
| **Tagline** | Patience pays. |
| **Brand promise** | Get the things you want, on purpose, without the guilt. |
| **Personality** | Warm, upbeat, and encouraging, like a friend with ADHD who's figured some things out. Playful, but never childish. |
| **Emotional goal** | Users feel anticipation and pride, not restriction and shame. |

**Voice guidelines**

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Celebrate wins, even small ones: "You kept $9 yesterday!" | Use "fix," "discipline," "cut back," or "get your act together" |
| Treat lapses as normal: "Welcome back!" | Use red, warnings, "overdue," or streak counts |
| Say "later," not "no": "Let's put it on the wishlist" | Treat wants as bad or wasteful |
| Keep words short and screens simple | Use dense paragraphs or financial jargon |
| Respect the user: "Your brain works differently" | Imply they're broken: "Your brain is the problem" |

*Name check: before finalizing, search app stores and the U.S. trademark database (USPTO) for conflicts, and test pronunciation, since "Patiencents" may be heard as "patients."*

## Step 7: Positioning Statement

### Template

**For audiences:**

- U.S. adults with ADHD, aged 50 and under, who use or want to try a budgeting app

**Our product solves the following problems:**

- Budgeting takes too much effort, so users abandon their tools within weeks
- Wants are handled with willpower or regret, neither of which works for ADHD brains
- Falling off track creates a backlog and guilt, so users quit for good

**Other businesses in the space:**

- **YNAB** teaches a proven budgeting method and markets to ADHD users, but its method depends on frequent manual effort, and its wish lists are manual categories.
- **PocketGuard** automates tracking and shows a safe-to-spend number, but doesn't connect restraint to future rewards or support ADHD users.
- **Rule** gamifies saving for ADHD users, but relies on resisting impulses in the moment, is early-stage and UK-based, and lacks core budgeting tools.
- **Inflow** teaches CBT-based ADHD skills and helps users recover from setbacks, but has no money features.

**Unlike other businesses, we:**

- turn daily patience into bank-confirmed progress toward the things users actually want, scheduling every wish for a Buy Day instead of asking users to resist, with a no-backlog design that makes coming back effortless.

### Final Positioning Statement

> 🎯 For U.S. adults with ADHD who want control over their money but keep abandoning budgeting apps, **Patiencents is the budgeting app that turns patience into the things you want.** It gives you one number for today, puts what you don't spend into your Patience Pot, and schedules a Buy Day for everything on your wishlist, so you get what you want, on purpose. And if you slip or step away, nothing piles up. Unlike YNAB, which depends on daily discipline, or PocketGuard, which only tracks what's already been spent, Patiencents rewards patience with real, bank-confirmed progress toward the things you actually want.
